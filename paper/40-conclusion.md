# Conclusion

This work set out to define superintelligence in terms a test can decide, to find the mechanisms
that would form it, and to derive from them a protocol that stops it while useful AI stays allowed
and improving. Part I defined the endpoint as beating a named human organisation at matched time and
money across the decisive clusters (D1), and the line as a breakout time shorter than the margin
times the regime's time to detect and respond (D4). It measured the mechanisms from five
perspectives and ranked seven pathways, with recursive self-improvement and undeclared compute tied
first. Part II derived one limit per mechanism, each with the channel that verifies it, what that
channel cannot see and what it costs useful AI, and a verification design that trusts named parties
for named things. Part III put twelve claims to six levels of verification.

The record so far is mostly negative. Four levels have run, the third in part; no claim has passed
all six, so none is yet a contribution, and none is new. Most of the protocol is prior work
assembled, and what may be ours is narrow: the margin as an error budget, a rule for merging
channels that share a source, the pathway order as a judgement, and two candidates still to face the
last two levels. Where experiments have reached a claim, they bear against it more often than for
it. Public data cannot identify the drift of algorithmic efficiency or compute's share of research
input. The loop-gain trigger cannot tell a loop below 1 from one above at the only noise calibrated
on data. A training cap leaks in jumps, not at the constant rate breakout tables assume. The margin
covers estimation error only, not the fall in breakout time between checks. The arms-control record
gives no bounded response time. Covert compute takes at least decades to find at the historical
discovery rate, and the coupled loops cannot be certified today. Neither side of the line is yet
measured where it matters most.

Four results would change that most. The first is whether the untraced pool can be closed; while it
is open, every party fails the line, and no experiment yet tests the steps that would shrink it: E22
bounds supply from above and cannot show the pool closing. The second is a drilled measurement of
the licensors' decision time, which no experiment yet plans. The third is a staged run on real
rounds (E4(b)), which decides whether the loop-gain estimator reopens or is cut. The fourth is the
elicitation experiment (E3), which decides whether rates per named budget hold up on organisms built
to hide capability. After them come Paperwright's desks and a reviewer who has not seen the work.

The protocol is a design. Nothing in it has been deployed or tested against a real adversary, and
the measurements so far show where it would fail at least as clearly as where it might hold. Every
number traces to a public ledger and every experiment reruns from public code, so each finding here
can be checked, and any of them overturned.
