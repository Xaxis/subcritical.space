# Conclusion

This work set out to define superintelligence in terms a test can decide, to find the mechanisms
that would form it, and to derive from them a protocol that stops it while useful AI stays allowed
and improving. The definitions and the protocol are assembled from credited prior work. What the
measurements add is mostly negative. Public data cannot identify the drift of algorithmic efficiency
or compute's share of research input. The loop-gain trigger cannot tell a loop below 1 from one above
at the only calibrated noise. A training cap leaks in jumps, not at a constant rate. The margin
covers estimation error only, not the fall in breakout time between checks. The arms-control record
gives no bounded response time, covert detection times are transfers decades long, and the coupled
loops cannot be certified today. The line is defined, but it can be evaluated only on the declared
path, and every party fails it through the untraced pool, cloud compute and distillation.

Four results would change that most: a test of whether the untraced pool can be closed, which no
experiment yet makes; a drilled measurement of the licensors' decision time; a staged run on real
rounds (E4(b)), which decides whether the loop-gain estimator reopens; and the elicitation
experiment (E3), which decides whether rates per named budget hold up on organisms built to hide
capability. Then Paperwright's desks.

The protocol is a design, deployed nowhere and tested against no real adversary. Every number
traces to a public ledger and every experiment reruns from public code, so each finding here can be
checked, and any of them overturned.
